## The Headline
The reality is, there's nothing really new here. Abusing S7Comm is not a new idea. No new vulnerability is being exploited to do this. The mitigations to these types of attacks have been implemented long ago by any automation engineers with a security hat in their back pocket.

But what is new, is using AI to create these attacks. Now, the CISA article is sparse of the details of how they know these scripts are being generate with AI, but I'm guessing the AI is supplementing a lack of experience to increase the pace these attacks can be created and evolved. Writing these sorts of attacks, especially with the convience of a dedicated library like 'python-snap7' isn't very hard.

## Top Mitigations from CISA
Below are the top recommended mitigations from CISA themselves:
- Inventory all Siemens S7 Series programmable logic controllers (PLCs)
  - A no brainer, but not really a mitigation in intself. We should all be doing this already. Having a reliable asset inventory makes your life ten fold easier. But just knowing you have an S7-1500 in your plant doesn't stop it being attacked.
- Apply critical security patches 
  - Yes, this is a good thing to do, but we need to appreciate it's not always feasible.
- Ensure PLCs are not accessible from the Internet
  - Now this one should *almost always* be feasible. I struggle to think of any reason you'd have your PLC exposed to the internet. There are *maybe* some scenarios where you'd want the web interface exposed for some *very, very* niche reasons, but even that isn't a great idea.
  - There is an ironic problem with this advise which I think is two fold:
    1. People who *purposfully* expose their PLCs to the internet are not reading CISA advisories, or any cyber security material.
    2. Parties with PLCs exposed to the internet probably **don't know** they're exposed to the internet. I'd hazard a guess most are exposed by accident via 3/4/5G modems or ISP links that shouldn't be there.
- Strengthen access controls
  - Always a good shout.
- Monitor for unauthorized activity
  - Easier said than done. You need people to actually do this work on a regular basis *and* a response plan if they see something strange, without risking disruption to service.
- Harden PLC services, protocols, and ladder logic integrity 
  - Of course
- Hunt for anomalies that may indicate a compromise
  - See 'Monitor for unauthorized activity'


## The Real Risk
I think I have to start making assumptions here, as there doesn't seem to be anything officially released on this, but the article makes clear mention of `snap7.dll` and the `python-snap7` library, leading us to believe these attacks are focused on the **S7Comm** protocol, which runs on TCP port 102.

S7Comm is well known to be insecure. On older S7 models (pre 1200), it's enabled by default, has no encryption or integrity checking, and allows for the read and writing of memory, as well as changing the running mode of the CPU.

But by default, S7-1200 and -1500 CPUs don't have S7Comm enabled. They, instead, have the new, improved, all singing, all dancing, super secure S7CommSecure. So what's the difference?

## How to *actually* mitigate these attacks
The advice from CISA is, on the whole, solid, but there are no practical steps given to carry this out. That's what I hope to cover here.
**DISCLAIMER: I only have a S7-1200 CPU in my lab, so all the advice is for that model. This should be fairly cut/paste for 1500s with will differ more for older models.**

### Inventory
Passive asset scanning is good. Active asset scanning is good. Walking around the factory floor is good. Do all three and you'll likely catch everything. Do only 1 and you'll probably miss some thins.

If you're looking for an active scanning tool, check [OTbase Snapshot](https://otbase.com/products/otbase-snapshot/) (not an ad, unfortunately!). This should pick up a lot of the OT assets on your site, including your S7s.

### Patching
Always easier said than done but put in the work and reap the rewards.

Siemens have [guidance](https://docs.tia.siemens.cloud/r/simatic_s7_1200_manual_collection_eses_20/online-and-diagnostic-tools/updating-firmware) online about performing firmware upgrades that is worth reviewing before you jump into the process.

Of course, the process of upgrading within your organisation will vary but having a good change control process is a must. Make sure you take your back ups, have a roll back plan, know what you're doing, etc etc.

Patching won't actually mitigate the attacks mentioned in this CISA article, but it may give you access to more security controls than you had before (e.g. v4 of the 1200 firmware added S7CommSecure).

### Internet Accessibility
If you need remote access or support for your PLC, don't expose it to the internet. Use a jump host. Use a VPN. Use a firewall. Use a commercial Secure Remote Access solution. *Even use teamviewer.* Of all the mitigations, this is the most important. Attacks love low hanging fruit. They won't try any harder than they have to to compelte their goals. 

The million dollar question, however, is this - *how do I know if my PLC is accessible from the internet?*

Here's a few ideas:

#### Internet Wide Scanning Services
Take the IP ranges you/your organisation own, jam them into shodan, filter for port 102, and analyse. Find something? Get it off of there.

Don't just stop at [Shodan](https://www.shodan.io/dashboard), try [Censys](https://search.censys.io) and [Netlas](https://netlas.io/) too.

If you have a standard naming convention (that is followed!) for your PLCs, you might be able to use that to hunt them down. If you're searching port 102 on these sites:

![](/blog/res/cisa-s7plc-plcname.png)

#### Asset Scanning/Inventory
Scoure your asset registers for anything that looks like an **ISP or 4G modem**. Employ active scanning to try and hunt these devices out in your sites. If you can, visit sites and do walk arounds looking for these things.

It's not an easy task, and there's isn't really one robust method to find shadow IT backdoor connections, but neglecting it could be how your sites get owned.

### Threat Hunting
I won't reinvent the wheel - 1898 have already written a very comprehensive [threat hunting guide](https://1898advisories.burnsmcd.com/threat-hunt-plan-siemens-s7-series-plcs-ai-assisted-s7comm-exploitation-snap7-masquerading-tools-and-internet-exposure). Use it to hunt and create detection rules for your environment. One key thing to highlight when you're analysing S7Comm traffic is below:
> "...TCP port 102 carries both S7comm and IEC 61850 MMS; the two are distinguished by the protocol-ID byte at the start of the COTP payload — 0x32 for classic S7comm, 0x72 for S7CommPlus, and neither for MMS — so a port-based query alone will conflate them at a site running both." - [The 1898 & Co. Team](https://1898advisories.burnsmcd.com/threat-hunt-plan-siemens-s7-series-plcs-ai-assisted-s7comm-exploitation-snap7-masquerading-tools-and-internet-exposure#:~:text=First)

## Final Thoughts
Yes, people are attacking OT systems. Yes, there is a real cyber security threat out there. Of course there is. We all know this. I think the awareness piece is done. Now we need practical help. Hardening guides. Threat Hunting guides. Detection rules.
